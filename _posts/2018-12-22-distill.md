# Vector Spaces

The definition of a vector space is often one of the first concepts introduced to students in linear algebra 101. Ironically, more often than not, students walk away from learning what a vector space is with a question "What is a vector space?".

<div class="box" markdown="1">
**Definition (Vector space).** A vector space is a collection $(V, \mathbb{F}, +, \cdot)$ where $V$ is a vector set, $\mathbb{F}$ is a field, $+ \:V \times V \to V$ is a vector addition operation, and $\cdot\:\mathbb{F} \times V \to V$ is a scalar vector multiplication operation such that the following properties hold:

1. Commutativity: $v+w = w+v$ for all $v, w \in V$;
2. Associativity: $(u+v)+w = u+(v+w)$ for all $v,w,u \in V$;
3. Zero vector: There exists $\vec{0} \in V$ such that $v + \vec{0} = v$ for all $v \in V$;
4. Additive inverse: For all $v \in V$, there exists a vector $w \in V$ such that $v + w = \vec{0}$. $w$ is usually denoted $-v$;
5. Multiplicative identity: $1v = v$ for all $v \in V$;
6. Multiplicative associativity: $(\alpha\beta)v = \alpha(\beta v)$ for all $\alpha,\beta \in \mathbb{F}$ and $v\in V$;
7. Distributivity: $(\alpha + \beta)v = \alpha v + \beta v$ and $\alpha(v + w) = \alpha v + \alpha w$ for all $\alpha,\beta \in \mathbb{F}$ and $v,w \in V$; 
</div>

<aside>
  <p>
    Different textbooks might have slightly different definition and number of axioms. For example, some might include axioms of closure under addition and multiplication where $v + w \in V$ and $\alpha v \in V$ for all $\alpha \in \mathbb{F}$ and $v,w \in V$. However, this is already implicitly implied by our definition of the addition and multiplication operations. Axiom of commutativity might not be included in some textbooks since it can be derived from other axioms etc.
  </p>
</aside>

So that's the definition of a vector space. It is not an arrow or a bunch of numbers standing on top of each other. What you are reading above is all what a vector space is, an abstract algebra structure. However, to truly understand what a vector space is, we need to understand why we are interested in studying vector spaces and where the definition comes from.

Consider the following problem. You want to keep track of your finance. That means you need to be able to know how much money you have, how much money you can spend before you have too much debt, how much money you need to buy a Steam Frame etc. A simple way to do this is to represent how much money you have with a real number. We make the following observations:

<div class="box" markdown="1">
1. Earning $x$ and then earning $y$ amount of money is the same as earning $y$ and then earning $x$;
2. Adding zero money does not change the amount of money you have;
</div>

<div class="box" markdown="1">
INCLUDE DRAWING OF THE HALLWAY
</div>

Consider another problem. You want to control and track a robot moving a long a thin one-dimensional hallway. Once again, you can use a real number to represent the distance of the robot from the center of the hallway. We make similar observations:

<div class="box" markdown="1">
1. Moving forward $x$ meters and then moving $y$ meters forward is the same as moving forward $y$ meters and then $x$ meters;
2. Moving $0$ meters is the same as staying at the same location;
</div>

<div class="box" markdown="1">
INCLUDE DRAWING OF PROFIT AND DEBT
</div>

Hopefully you can see that the behavior of the set of real numbers we use along with elementary additon in both problems are the same. So, in order to generalise our solution of these problems to other similar problems, we abstract away the context of the problem and we arrive at the following axioms of elementary algebra.

<div class="box" markdown="1">
1. $x + y$ = $y+x$ for all $x,y \in \mathbb{R}$
2. $x + 0 = x$ for all $x \in \mathbb{R}$;
</div>

What if instead of just moving the robot along a hallway, you want to move and track the robot around your entire house. The only difference with the previous example is that instead of using $\mathbb{R}$, we now use $\mathbb{R^2}$ (assuming the robot is not flying around the house). It turns out that the algebraic behaviour in this two-dimensional case is quite similar to what we had before.

<div class="box" markdown="1">
1. $x + y$ = $y+x$ for all $x,y \in \mathbb{R^2}$
2. $x + 0 = x$ for all $x \in \mathbb{R^2}$;
</div>

<div class="box" markdown="1">
INCLUDE DRAWING OF a 2D ROOM PLAN
</div>

Instead of limiting ourselves to a specific dimension, we generalize the above axioms to arbitrary n-dimensional space.

<div class="box" markdown="1">
1. $x + y$ = $y+x$ for all $x,y \in \mathbb{R^n}$
2. $x + 0 = x$ for all $x \in \mathbb{R^n}$;
</div> 

Astute readers will notice that the above axioms are very similar to axiom 1 and 3 of our definition of vector spaces. That is because the set of $\mathbb{R^n}$, along with elementwise addition and scalar multiplication over $\mathbb{R}$ is a special case of vector space. One could think of the definition of vector space as a generalization of $(\mathbb{R^n}, \mathbb{R}, +, \cdot)$. This means that any techniques and theories we developed for vector spaces can be used to solve, not only problems in any $\mathbb{R^n}$, but also for anythings that satisfy the axioms. However, in this article, we will limit ourselves to $\mathbb{R^n}$ and the field $\mathbb{R}$ for simplicity.

Now that we have established why we are intersted in vector spaces, the definition of a subspace is a lot easier to digest.

<div class="box" markdown="1">
  **Definition (Subspace).** Let $V$ be a subspace. A subspace $W \subset V$ is a non-emtpy subset of $V$ such that $W$ is also a vector space. 
</div>

<aside>
  <p>
    In some textbooks, the definition may include the requirement that $W$ need to be closed under addition and multiplication ($\alpha v + \beta u \in W$ for all $u,v \in W$). However, this is equivalent to requiring $W$ to be a vector space. That is because all the axioms of a vector is already satisfied by inheriting the elements and opertations of vector space $V$. The only thing left that may not be inherited is being closed under addition and multiplication.
  </p>
</aside>

In computer vision, we often deal with two sets of vector spaces. The vector space $\mathbb{R^3}$ representing the 3D world and a 2D subset of $\mathbb{R^3}$ representing the image or projection of 3D objects onto 2D image plane. The idea of subspace allow us to treat the 2D image plane projection as its own 2-dimensional vector space which is a lot more convenient to work with. We will explore why this is more convenient further when we get to the topics of bases and coordinates.

# Linear Combination, Span, Spanning Set and Bases

<div class="box" markdown="1">
**Definition (Linear combination).** Let $V$ be a vector space over a field $\mathbb{F}$ and let $v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n \in V$. A linear combination of $v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n$ is a vector of the form

$$
\alpha_1 v_1 + \alpha_2 v_2 + \dots + \alpha_n v_n = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \alpha_i v_i,
$$

where $\alpha_1, \alpha_2, \dots, \alpha_n \in \mathbb{F}$. The scalars $\alpha_i$ are called the coefficients of the linear combination.
</div>

From the axioms of vector spaces being closed under addition and multiplication, we know that a linear combination of any set of vectors will give you another vector in the same vector space. This means that from a fixed set of vectors, we can generate a "bigger" set of vectors by considering different sets of coefficients. This gives us the notion of span.

<div class="box" markdown="1">
**Definition (Span).** Let $V$ be a vector space over a field $\mathbb{F}$ and let $v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n \in V$. The span of $v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n$, denoted $\operatorname{span}(v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n)$, is the set of all linear combinations of $v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n$:

$$
\operatorname{span}(v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n) = \left\lbrace \sum_{i=1}^{n} \alpha_i v_i : \alpha_1, \alpha_2, \dots, \alpha_n \in \mathbb{F} \right\rbrace.
$$

We say that $v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n$ span $\operatorname{span}(v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n)$. By convention, the span of the empty list is $\lbrace 0 \rbrace$.
</div>

For example, the vector $[1, 0, 0] \in \mathbb{R^3}$ span the line $\lbrace [\alpha, 0, 0] : \alpha \in \mathbb{R} \rbrace$ and the vectors $[1,0,0], [0, 1, 0] \in \mathbb{R^3}$ span the plane $\lbrace [\alpha, \beta, 0]: \alpha,\beta \in \mathbb{R} \rbrace$.

<div class="box" markdown=1>
INSERT DRAWING
</div>

A good question to ask is "Does there exist a set of vectors in $V$ that span $V$ itself?". The answer is yes, and such sets are called spanning sets. For example, all vector spaces are spanning sets of themselves.

<div class="box" markdown="1">
**Definition (Spanning set).** Let $V$ be a vector space over a field $\mathbb{F}$. A subset $S \subseteq V$ is a spanning set of $V$ if every vector in $V$ is a linear combination of vectors in $S$, that is, if

$$
\operatorname{span}(S) = V.
$$
</div>

<aside>
  <p>
  For simplicty, we limit ourselves to finite-dimensional vector spaces, which are vector spaces $V$ where there exist $S \subseteq V$ such that $\operatorname{span}(S) = V$ and $\lvert S \rvert$ is finite. A good example of finite-dimensional vector spaces are the $\mathbb{R^n}$ vector spaces. For example, $\lbrace [1,0], [0,1] \rbrace$ spans $\mathbb{R^2}$.
  </p>
</aside>

It's clear that, given a vector space $V$, spanning sets are not unique and they may not even have the same number of vectors. For example, both $$ A = \left\lbrace \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \right\rbrace$$ and $$ B = \left\lbrace \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \right\rbrace$$ span the same vector space $\mathbb{R^2}$. However, we note that the linear combination coefficients to create any vector in $\mathbb{R^2}$ is unique with $A$ but not unique with $B$. Consider vector $$\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R^2}$$. With $A$, we can only generate $$\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}$$ with exactly $$2\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$$ and $$3\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$$. With $B$, we can have $$2\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$$, $$3\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$$ and $$0\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$$, or $$0\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$$, $$1\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$$ and $$2\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$$. In fact, the possible combination for $B$ to create $$\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}$$ is infinite. This is because any vector in $B$ belongs to the span of the other two vectors. To see why this gives you infinite number of combination, start with the solution $$2\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$$, $$3\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$$ and $$0\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$$, for every one of $$\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$$ we add, we can subtract one of each $$\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$$ and $$\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$$ to keep the linear combination unchanged. We say the vectors in A are linearly independent and the vectors in B are linearly dependent. Intuitively, we can think of a spanning set as a set that contain enough information to generate any vector in but the set may contain redundant information if it is linearly dependent.



<div class="box" markdown=1>
INSERT GRID DRAWING
</div>

<div class="box" markdown="1">
**Definition (Linear dependence).** Let $V$ be a vector space over a field $\mathbb{F}$ and let $v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n \in V$. The vectors $v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n$ are linearly dependent if there exist $\alpha_1, \alpha_2, \dots, \alpha_n \in \mathbb{F}$, not all zero, such that

$$
\alpha_1 v_1 + \alpha_2 v_2 + \dots + \alpha_n v_n = 0.
$$

Otherwise, they are linearly independent, that is, $\sum_{i=1}^{n} \alpha_i v_i = 0$ implies $\alpha_1 = \alpha_2 = \dots = \alpha_n = 0$.
</div>

<aside>
  <p>
    This definition of linear dependence is equivalent to saying any vector in the set is in the span of the other vectors. To see why, when not all coefficients are zero, we can  have $-\alpha_j v_j = \sum_{i \neq j} \alpha_i v_i$. Picking $j$ with $\alpha_j \neq 0$ and dividing both sides by $-\alpha_j$ gives $v_j = \sum_{i \neq j} -\frac{\alpha_i}{\alpha_j} v_i$.
  </p>
</aside>

A nice property of a linear independent set is that the corresponding coefficients for each vector in its span are unique.

<div class="box" id="theorem-uniqueness-of-coefficients" markdown="1">
**Theorem (Uniqueness of coefficients).** Let $V$ be a vector space over a field $\mathbb{F}$ and let $v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n \in V$ be linearly independent. Then every $v \in \operatorname{span}(v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n)$ can be written as a linear combination of $v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n$ in exactly one way. That is, if

$$
v = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \alpha_i v_i = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \beta_i v_i,
$$

then $\alpha_i = \beta_i$ for all $i = 1, 2, \dots, n$.

Conversely, if $v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n$ are linearly dependent, then every $v \in \operatorname{span}(v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n)$ can be written as a linear combination of $v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n$ in infinitely many ways.
</div>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Proof</summary>

Suppose $v = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \alpha_i v_i = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \beta_i v_i$. Subtracting the two expressions gives

$$
0 = \sum_{i=1}^{n} (\alpha_i - \beta_i) v_i.
$$

Since $v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n$ are linearly independent, every coefficient must be zero, so $\alpha_i - \beta_i = 0$, that is, $\alpha_i = \beta_i$ for all $i$.

For the linearly dependent case, start with any linear combination $v = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \alpha_i v_i$. Since $v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n$ are linearly dependent, some vector $v_j$ is in the span of the others, that is, $v_j = \sum_{i \neq j} c_i v_i$ for some $c_i \in \mathbb{F}$. Now, for every $t \in \mathbb{F}$, subtract $t$ from the coefficient of $v_j$ and make it up by adding $t c_i$ to the coefficient of each other $v_i$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
v &= \sum_i\alpha_i v_i \\
&= \alpha_j v_j + \sum_{i \neq j}\alpha_i v_i \\
&=(\alpha_j - t) v_j + \sum_{i \neq j} (\alpha_i + t c_i) v_i \\
&= \sum_{i} \alpha_i v_i - t v_j + t \sum_{i \neq j} c_i v_i \\
&= v - t v_j + t v_j \\
&= v.
\end{aligned}
$$

Since each $t$ gives a different set of coefficients, this implies the number of possible coefficients are infinite. $\blacksquare$

</details>


Intuitively, linear indepdence implies that all vector coefficients contribute some unique information to the linear combination that no other coefficient have. So for each vector, there exist only one way to contribute. For example, in $\operatorname{span}(A)$, the coefficient of $$\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$$ tells you where $$\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}$$ is along the first axis and the coefficient of $$\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$$ tells you where it is on the second axis. On the other hand, in $\operatorname{span}(B)$, the locations along the two axes are "entangled" with $$\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$$ which means there are infinitely many different way to combine them. 

However, if we remove any one vector from $B$, $B$ becomes linearly independent while remaining a spanning set. Because of how useful a set that is both a spanning set and being linearly independent is, we have a name for it.

<div class="box" markdown="1">
**Definition (Basis).** Let $V$ be a vector space over a field $\mathbb{F}$. A list of vectors $v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n \in V$ is a basis of $V$ if

1. $v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n$ span $V$, and
2. $v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n$ are linearly independent.
</div>

So if a spanning set is a set with enough information (potentially redundant in case of being linearly dependent) to generate the vector space, a linearly independent set is a set where each vector contribute some unique information (but potentially not enough to generate the set in case of being non-spanning), then a basis can be thought of a set that has exactly just enough information to generate the vector space, no more, no less. 

We can start from any arbitrary linearly dependent spanning set and remove redundant information (linearly dependent vectors) until it become a basis. Once it becomes a basis, removing any more vector will make it a linearly independent non-spanning set since all vectors in a basis contribute some unique information about the vector space. Another way of saying the above is, given a linearly dependent spanning set $S$, we can find a smaller subset basis $B$ and linearly independent non-spanning $I$ such that $I \subset B \subset S$ and $\lvert I \rvert < \lvert B \rvert < \lvert S \rvert$. In fact, we can have an even stronger result regarding the size of spanning set and linearly independent set in general.

<div class="box" markdown="1">
**Theorem (Linearly independent sets are no larger than spanning sets).** Let $V$ be a finite-dimensional vector space over a field $\mathbb{F}$. If $u_1, u_2, \dots, u_m \in V$ are linearly independent and $w_1, w_2, \dots, w_n \in V$ span $V$, then $m \leq n.$
</div>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Proof</summary>

Assume that $m > n$. We perform the following steps $n$ times from $k=1$ to $k=n$.

1. Move $u_k$ from $U = \lbrace u_k,\dots, u_m \rbrace$ to $W = \lbrace u_1, \dots, u_{k-1}, w_k, \dots, w_n \rbrace$. 
2. Since $W$ is spanning, we can write $\sum_{i=1}^{k-1} \alpha_i u_i + \sum_{j=k}^n \beta_j w_j = u_k$. Since $\lbrace u_1, \dots, u_k \rbrace$ is linearly independent, there must exist a $\beta_j \neq 0$. We can remove $w_j$ while keeping $W$ spanning.
3. Reindex the remaining $w$ from $k+1$ to $n$.

However, for $k = n+1$, we have a spanning set $W = \lbrace u_1, u_2, \dots, u_n\rbrace$. This implies that $u_{n+1}, \dots, u_m$ can be generated by $u_1, \dots, u_n$ which is a contradiction.

</details>

This result implies all bases in a vector space have the same number of vectors (can you see why?) and leads to the following definition of the dimension of a vector space.

<div class="box" markdown="1">
**Definition (Dimension).** Let $V$ be a finite-dimensional vector space over a field $\mathbb{F}$. The dimension of $V$, denoted $\dim V$, is the number of vectors in any basis of $V$.
</div>

<aside>
  <p>
    Using this definition, we can check that for any vector space $\mathbb{R^n}$, the dimension must be $n$ since we can form a simple standard basis of size $n$. Neat!
  </p>
</aside>

Since a basis is both a linearly independent set and a spanning set, we can represent any vector in a vector space $V$ with a unique linear combination of the basis. This means that we can use the linear combination coefficient as a coordinate w.r.t. a basis. For example the coordinate of $$\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}$$ is $(2,3)$ w.r.t. the standard basis $$\lbrace \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \rbrace$$ and $(1, 2)$ w.r.t. $$\lbrace \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \rbrace$$. However, why should we be interested in different coordinate systems?

Suppose you live in a residential area whose streets form a grid, but the grid is tilted relative to north and east. A visitor asks you how to get to the nearest train station. You could tell them that the station is about 160 metres east and 323 metres north of where you are standing. That is accurate, but not very helpful, since they cannot walk through houses in a straight line. It is far more useful to say "walk 3 blocks along this street, then turn left and walk 2 blocks". Both answers describe the same vector, just with respect to different bases. The first uses the compass directions east and north, while the second uses the directions of the two streets. The second basis fits the problem, so its coordinates are the ones the visitor can actually use.

Basis also allow us to simplify the representation of lower dimensional subspace and its vectors. Consider the previous example of 2D projection of 3D objects in computer vision. Let the top middle of the image to be $$\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$$ and the right middle of the image be $$\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$$. Clearly, $$\lbrace \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \rbrace$$ is a basis for our image plane that contains our image. For example, the top right corner of our image can be written as $$ \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$$. However, representing vector on a 2 dimensional subspace with vectors in $\mathbb{R^3}$ does not seem that elegant. Instead, we can represent the coordinate of the corner in the 2 dimensional subspace image plane as $(1,1)$ w.r.t. $$\lbrace \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \rbrace$$. Additionally, when we rotate our camera, the positions on the image in 3D may change but it is irrelevant to consumer of the image. For example, say the image is rotated clockwise so that the top and the right of the image are now at $$\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$$ and $$\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ -2 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$$ respectively. The top right corner is now at $$\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -2 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$$, a different location in 3D space. However, the coordinate of the top right corner w.r.t. $$\lbrace \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ -2 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \rbrace$$ remain at $(1,1)$.

Hopefully, you can appreciate why we are interested in basis of vector spaces. In fact, the rotated camera example we just gave is a very good motivator as to why we are interested in studying linear transformations.


# Linear Transformations and Matrices

So far, we have discussed various properties of vector spaces. However, in the study of linar algebra, we are also interestd in the study of a class of transformations that preserve the structure of vector spaces called linear transformation. In this section, we discuss the "linear" part in linear algebra and show how matrices can be used to represent linear transformations.

In the rotated camera example, before the rotation, the top right corner of the image was the sum of the top and the right of the image. After the rotation, it is still the sum of the new top and the new right, which is why its coordinate stayed at $(1,1)$. In other words, adding then rotating gives the same result as rotating then adding. The same holds for scalar multiplication: the point halfway to the top of the image is still halfway to the new top after the rotation. In fact, performing any arbitrary linear combination before the rotation is the same as performing the same linear combination after. For example, all the points on a line still lie on a line after the rotation, and all the points on the image plane still lie on a plane, namely the rotated image plane. Informally, we say the rotation preserve the structure of the 3D space. In fact, rotation belongs to a class of structure preserving transformation we call linear transformation.

<div class="box" markdown="1">
**Definition (Linear transformation/linear mapping).** Let $V$ and $W$ be vector spaces over the same field $\mathbb{F}$. A function $T : V \to W$ is a linear transformation if

1. $T(u + v) = T(u) + T(v)$ for all $u, v \in V$, and
2. $T(\alpha v) = \alpha T(v)$ for all $\alpha \in \mathbb{F}$ and $v \in V$.
</div>

<aside>
  <p>
    Properties 1 and 2 together are equivalent to the single property $T(\alpha u + \beta v) = \alpha T(u) + \beta T(v)$ for all $u, v \in V$ and $\alpha, \beta \in \mathbb{F}$.
  </p>
</aside>

To see how linear transformations preserve structure of vector spaces, let $\lbrace u_1, \dots, u_n \rbrace \subset V$ be a basis of $V$ and $T: V \to W$ being a linear transformation. Then we have $$T(\sum_i\alpha_iu_i) = \sum_i\alpha_iT(u_i)$$. This means that the coordinate w.r.t. $\lbrace u_1, \dots, u_n \rbrace$ of any $v \in V$ is the same as the coordinate w.r.t. $\lbrace T(u_1), \dots, T(u_n)\rbrace$ of any $w \in W$.

For example, let $v$ be a vector and $\alpha \in \mathbb{R}$. Then all the vectors on the line $\alpha v$ will be mapped to all the points on the line $\alpha T(v)$ for any linear mapping $T$. In general, given a set of vectors $\lbrace v_i\rbrace$, the $\operatorname{span}(\lbrace v_i\rbrace)$ will be mapped to $\operatorname{span}(\lbrace T(v_i) \rbrace)$ for any linear mapping $T$.

<div class="box" markdown=1>
INSERT DRAWING
</div>

One observation we can make is that to figure out the mapping $T(v)$ of any vector $v \in V$ under any linear mapping $T: V \to W$, we only need to know the mapping $\lbrace T(u_i) \rbrace \subset W$ for a generating set $\lbrace u_i\rbrace \subset V$. That is because for any $v\in V$, we have

$$
T(v) = T(\sum_i \alpha_i u_i) = \sum_i \alpha_i T(u_i).
$$

In fact, the mapping $\lbrace T(u_i) \rbrace$ uniquely defines $T$. Consider $T_1, T_2: V \to W$ such that $T_1(u_i) = T_2(u_i)$ for any $u_i$. For any $v \in V$, we have

$$
T_1(v) = \sum_i \alpha_i T_1(u_i) = \sum_i \alpha_i T_2(u_i) = T_2(v),
$$

which is another way of saying $T_1 = T_2$.

Let our generating set to be the standard basis vector $\lbrace e_i \rbrace \subset \mathbb{F}^n$. We have

$$
T(v) = T(\alpha_1e_1 + \cdots + \alpha_ne_n) = \alpha_1T(e_1) + \cdots + \alpha_nT(e_n).
$$

Create a matrix $M \in \mathbb{F^{m \times n}}$ where the $i\text{th}$ column of $M$ is $T(e_i)$ and a vector $a \in \mathbb{F^n}$ where the $i\text{th}$ element of $a$ is $\alpha_i \in F$. Recall the definition of a matrix vector multiplication, we can see its relation with linear mapping as follows.

$$
T(v)
= \alpha_1 T(e_1) + \cdots + \alpha_n T(e_n) =
\begin{bmatrix}
\vert & & \vert \\
T(e_1) & \cdots & T(e_n) \\
\vert & & \vert
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
\alpha_1 \\
\vdots \\
\alpha_n
\end{bmatrix}
= Ma.
$$

This implies that we can represent the mapping $T(v)$ of any vector $v$ by $Ma$. We say $T$ is equivalent to $M$. Moving forward, we use $T$ (uppercase without brackets) to represent both a linear mapping and its equivalent matrix. In cases where we need to differentiate the two representation, we use $T$ to represent the matrix and $T()$ to represent the mapping.

The idea of a matrix representating a linear transformation allows us to connect the idea of matrix multiplication with the composition of multiple linear transformations. Let $T_1(): \mathbb{F^n} \to \mathbb{F^m}, T_2(): \mathbb{F^m} \to \mathbb{F^k}$ be linear transformations and $T_1 \in \mathbb{F^{m\times n}}, T_2 \in \mathbb{F^{k\times m}}$ be the equivalent matrices respectively. We are interested in the equivalent matrix $T = T_2T_1 \in \mathbb{F}^{k\times n}$ of the composition $T_2() \circ T_1(): \mathbb{F^n} \to \mathbb{F^k}$. Let $\lbrace e_i \rbrace$ be the standard basis in $\mathbb{F}^n$. For any vector $v\in \mathbb{F}^n$, we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
T(v) &= (T_2 \circ T_1)(v) \\
&= T_2(T_1(v)) \\
&= T_2(T_1(\sum_i\alpha_ie_i)) \\
&= T_2(\sum_i\alpha_iT_1(e_i)) \\
&= \sum_i\alpha_iT_2(T_1(e_i)).
\end{aligned}
$$

This means that the $i\text{th}$ column of $T$ need to be $T_2(T_1(e_i))$ which is equivalent to the mapping under $T_2()$ of the $i\text{th}$ column of $T_1$. Letting $T_1^i$ be the $i\text{th}$ column of $T_1$, $T$ can be constructed as follows.

$$
T = T_2 T_1 =
\begin{bmatrix}
\vert & \vert & & \vert \\
T_2 T_1^1 & T_2 T_1^2 & \cdots & T_2 T_1^n \\
\vert & \vert & & \vert
\end{bmatrix}
$$

Similarly, we can construct the equivalent matrix $T\in \mathbb{F}^{m\times n}$ of $T_1() + T_2()$ for $T_1(), T_2(): \mathbb{F}^n \to \mathbb{F}^m$. The $i\text{th}$ column of $T = T_1 + T_2$ must be $T_1(e_i) + T_2(e_i)$. We have

$$
T = T_1 + T_2 =
\begin{bmatrix}
\vert & & \vert \\
T_1^1 & \cdots & T_1^n \\
\vert & & \vert
\end{bmatrix}
+
\begin{bmatrix}
\vert & & \vert \\
T_2^1 & \cdots & T_2^n \\
\vert & & \vert
\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
\vert & & \vert \\
T_1^1 + T_2^1 & \cdots & T_1^n + T_2^n \\
\vert & & \vert
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

As we can see, seemingly arbitrary definition of matrix multiplication and addition can be easily derived by treating them as linear mappings.

For some linear mapping $T(): V \to W$, there clearly exists a $T^{-1}(): W \to V$ such that $T^{-1}(T(v)) = v$ for all $v\in V$ or $T^{-1}T = I$. For example, let $T$ be a clockwise rotation around the third axis in $\mathbb{R}^3$ when looking in the positive direction. Clearly, $T^{-1}$ is the rotation around the same axis but in the opposite direction with the same angle of rotation. In this case, we call $T^{-1}$ a left inverse of $T$ and $T$ a right inverse of $T^{-1}$.

<div class="box" markdown="1">
**Definition (Left and right inverse).** Let $V$ and $W$ be vector spaces over a field $\mathbb{F}$ and let $T : V \to W$ be a linear transformation.

1. A linear transformation $S : W \to V$ is a left inverse of $T$ if $S(T(v)) = v$ for all $v \in V$.
2. A linear transformation $R : W \to V$ is a right inverse of $T$ if $T(R(w)) = w$ for all $w \in W$.

If $T$ has both a left inverse and a right inverse, we say that $T$ is invertible.
</div>

<aside>
  <p>
    In the rotation example above, $T^{-1}$ is both a left and right inverse of $T$. Therefore $T$ is invertible. In fact, $T^{-1}$ is the unique inverse of $T$.
  </p>
</aside>

<div class="box" markdown="1">
**Theorem (Uniqueness of inverse).** Let $T : V \to W$ be an invertible linear transformation with a left inverse $S : W \to V$ and a right inverse $R : W \to V$. Then $S = R$. We call $S$ the inverse of $T$, denoted $T^{-1}$.
</div>

<aside>
  <p>
    Can you see why the inverse is unique?
  </p>
</aside>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Proof</summary>

For any $w \in W$, since $R$ is a right inverse of $T$, we have $T(R(w)) = w$. Applying $S$ to both sides and using that $S$ is a left inverse of $T$, we get

$$
S(w) = S(T(R(w))) = R(w).
$$

Hence, $S = R$. $\blacksquare$

</details>

The notions of left inverse matrix, right inverse matrix and inverse matrix are similar. We provide their definitions for completeness as follows.

<div class="box" markdown="1">
**Definition (Left, right and inverse matrix).** Let $A \in \mathbb{F}^{m \times n}$ be a matrix and let $I_k \in \mathbb{F}^{k \times k}$ denote the identity matrix, which has $1$ on the diagonal and $0$ everywhere else.

1. A matrix $B \in \mathbb{F}^{n \times m}$ is a left inverse of $A$ if $BA = I_n$.
2. A matrix $C \in \mathbb{F}^{n \times m}$ is a right inverse of $A$ if $AC = I_m$.

If $A$ has both a left inverse $B$ and a right inverse $C$, we say that $A$ is invertible. In this case, $B = C$, and we call it the inverse matrix of $A$, denoted $A^{-1}$.
</div>

When would a mapping/matrix have right/left inverses? Before answering that question, we introduce the following concepts.

<div class="box" markdown="1">
**Definition (Injective, surjective and bijective).** Let $X$ and $Y$ be sets and let $f : X \to Y$ be a mapping.

1. $f$ is injective (or one-to-one) if $f(x_1) = f(x_2)$ implies $x_1 = x_2$ for all $x_1, x_2 \in X$. 
2. $f$ is surjective (or onto) if for every $y \in Y$, there exists $x \in X$ such that $f(x) = y$.
3. $f$ is bijective if it is both injective and surjective.
</div>

<aside>
  <p>
    Note that we specifically use the term sets over vector spaces because the concept of injective, surjective and bijective are much more general than mapping between vector spaces.
  </p>
</aside>

Intuitively, we can think of injective mappings as mapping where the output of each input is unique but not every element of the codomain can be an output, surjective mappings as mapping where the set of possible outputs completely cover the codomain but multiple inputs may have the same output, and bijective mapping as mapping where for every elements in the domain, there exists a unique correspondence in the codomain and vice versa.

Now we can answer our question. Suppose $T : V \to W$ is injective, as in the left diagram. Every output $w = T(v)$ comes from exactly one input $v$, so we can undo $T$ by sending $w$ back to that $v$. For the elements of $W$ that $T$ never reaches, it does not matter where we send them, since a left inverse $S$ only needs to satisfy $S(T(v)) = v$. Hence, an injective mapping has a left inverse. A bit more formally, injectivity implies that we can form a one-to-one correspondence between a basis of $V$ with a linearly indepdent set of $W$. To construct a left inverse, we simply need to map each element of this independent set in $W$ to the correspondening basis in $V$. Clearly, the equivalent matrix of an injective mapping that is not surjective is a rectangle matrix $\mathbb{F}^{m\times n}$ where $n < m$ and its $n$ columns are linearly independent but do not span $\mathbb{F}^m$.

Suppose instead that $T$ is surjective, as in the middle diagram. Every $w \in W$ is the output of at least one input, so we can define $R(w)$ to be any one of them. Then $T(R(w)) = w$ for all $w \in W$, so $R$ is a right inverse of $T$. A bit more formally, surjectivity implies that we can form many-to-one correspondence between a spanning set n $V$ to a basis in $W$. By removing linearly independent elements in the spanning set until it becomes a basis in $V$, we can construct a right inverse from the one-to-one correspondence between the basis in $W$ and the basis leftover in $V$. Clearly, the equivalent matrix of a surjective mapping that is not injective is a rectangle matrix $\mathbb{F}^{m \times n}$ where $n > m$ and the columns are linearly dependent but span $\mathbb{F}^m$.

When $T$ is bijective, as in the right diagram, the construction for the left and right inverse is trivial due to unique correspondences between the two vector spaces. Notice that the constructions for the left and right inverses in the case of injective and surjective mappings involve a choice of how to map the basis and independent sets with each other. Hence they are not unique. When $T$ is bijective, there is no choice to make so the left and right inverse are the same and unique. The equivalent matrix for a bijective mapping must be a square matrix $\mathbb{F}^{n \times n}$ where its columns form a basis in $\mathbb{F}^n$.

One final note on the dimension of the domain, codomain and the matrices. Intuitively, a mapping from lower dimension to higher dimension can not cover all of the codomain and so it can not be surjective. A mapping from higher dimension to lower dimension can not be injective since it has too many elements in the domain. Therefore, the only way for a bijective mapping to exist is if the mapping stays in the same dimension. Hence, an equivalent matrix of a bijective mapping, which is also an invertible matrix, need to be a square matrix.

# Solutions of Ax=b

Given a matrix $A \in \mathbb{F}^{m \times n}$ and a vector $b \in \mathbb{F}^m$, solving for $x\in \mathbb{F}^n$ such that $Ax=b$ is one of the most important problems in applied mathematics. Many problems in the real world can be solved by reformulating the problem into the form of $Ax=b$ and solving it using the tools of linear algebra.

For example, you want to buy sushi. You want exactly 10 pieces of tuna sushi and 4 pieces of salmon sushi. However, the supermarket only sells two types of sushi plates, plates of 2 tuna sushi, or plates of 3 tuna and 2 salmon sushi. How many of each of these types should you buy? Is it possible to buy exactly the amount of sushi you want?

We can formulate the problem as a linear system of equations as follows. Solve for $x,y\in \mathbb{R}$ (for simplicity, assume we can buy fractional or negative number of plates) such that

$$
\begin{aligned}
2x + 3y &= 10 \\
0x + 2y &= 4
\end{aligned}
$$

This is equivalent to solving $A\begin{bmatrix}x \\ y\end{bmatrix}=b$ where

$$
A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 3 \\ 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix}, \quad
b = \begin{bmatrix} 10 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

One interesting remark we can make is that $A$ represents a linear mapping from the number of each type of plates you buy to the number of individual type of sushi those plates contain. For this specific problem, there exists a unique solution of $x=2, y=2$. On the other hand, if the types of plate being sold are plates of 2 tuna and 1 salmon, and plates of 4 tuna and 2 salmon, a solution would not exist. 

In this section, we are interested in finding the conditions for a solution to exist and the conditions for the solution to be unique. Everything we have discussed until now culminate into the following equivalent conditions.

<div class="box" markdown="1">
**Theorem (Injectivity).** Let $T(): \mathbb{F}^n \to \mathbb{F}^m$ be a linear mapping and $T\in \mathbb{F}^{m\times n}$ be the equivalent matrix where $n \leq m$. The following conditions are equivalent.

1. Columns of $T$ are linearly independent.
1. $T()$ is injective.
1. $T$ has a left inverse.
1. For every $b\in \mathbb{F}^m$, $Tx=b$ has at most one solution $x$.
</div>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Proof</summary>

**$1 \Rightarrow 2$.** By the [uniqueness of coefficients](#theorem-uniqueness-of-coefficients), it's clear that $T()$ is injective.

**$2 \Rightarrow 3$** Since $T()$ is injective, there is a one-to-one correspondence between a basis $B = \lbrace b_1, \cdots, b_n \rbrace$ in $\mathbb{F}^n$ to a linearly indendent set $U = \lbrace u_1, \cdots, u_n \rbrace$ of size $n$ in $\mathbb{F}^m$. We can construct a basis in $\mathbb{F}^m$ by adding more linearly indepdent vectors $u_{n+1}, \cdots, u_m$ to this set until it becomes a basis. We can construct a linear map $T^{-1}()$ by mapping each of the original vector in $U$ $u_1, \cdots, u_n$ to the corresponding vectors in the basis $B$ $b_1, \cdots, b_n$ while mapping the newly added vectors $u_{n+1}, \cdots, u_m$ to 0. For any vector $v\in \mathbb{F}^n$, we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
T^{-1}(T(v)) &= T^{-1}(T(\sum_i \alpha_i b_i)) \\
&= T^{-1}(\sum_i \alpha_i T(b_i)) \\
&= \sum_i \alpha_i T^{-1}(T(b_i)) \\
&= \sum_i \alpha_i T^{-1}(u_i) \\
&= \sum_i \alpha_i b_i \\
&= v
\end{aligned}
$$

$3 \Rightarrow 4$ Let $T^{-1}$ be a left inverse of $T$. Let $x,y\in \mathbb{F}^n$ be vectors such that $Tx=b$ and $Ty=b$. We have $x = T^{-1}Tx = T^{-1}b = T^{-1}Ty = y$.

$4 \Rightarrow 1$ Consider $b = 0$. Then there exists at most one $x$ such that $Tx = 0$. There is the trivial case of $x=0$. However, since there is at most one solution, by definition, columns of $T$ must be linearly independent.

$\blacksquare$

</details>

<div class="box" markdown="1">
**Theorem (Surjectivity).** Let $T(): \mathbb{F}^n \to \mathbb{F}^m$ be a linear mapping and $T\in \mathbb{F}^{m\times n}$ be the equivalent matrix where $n \geq m$. The following conditions are equivalent.

1. Columns of $T$ span $\mathbb{F}^m$.
1. $T()$ is surjective.
1. $T$ has a right inverse.
1. For every $b\in \mathbb{F}^m$, $Tx=b$ has at least one solution $x$.
</div>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Proof</summary>

$1 \Rightarrow 2$ This is trivial.

$2 \Rightarrow 3$ Let $B = \lbrace b_1, \cdots, b_m \rbrace$ be a basis in $\mathbb{F}^n$, $U = \lbrace T(b_1), \cdots, T(b_m) \rbrace = \lbrace u_1, \cdots, u_m\rbrace$ be the mapping of a subset of $B$. Clearly, $U$ is a basis in $\mathbb{F}^m$. We construct a mapping $T^{-1}()$ such that $T^{-1}(u_i) = b_i$. For any vector $v\in \mathbb{F}^n$, we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
T(T^{-1}(v)) &= T(T^{-1}(\sum_i \alpha_i u_i)) \\
&=T(\sum_i \alpha_i T^{-1}(u_i)) \\
&= \sum_i \alpha_i T(T^{-1}(u_i)) \\
&= \sum_i \alpha_i T(b_i) \\
&= \sum_i \alpha_i u_i \\
&= v
\end{aligned}
$$

$3 \Rightarrow 4$ Let $T^{-1}$ be a right inverse of $T$. For any $b\in \mathbb{F}^m$, we can find a solution to $Tx = b$ by choosing $x = T^{-1}(b)$.

$4 \Rightarrow 1$ This is trivial. 

</details>

<div class="box" markdown="1">
**Theorem (Bijectivity).** Let $T(): \mathbb{F}^n \to \mathbb{F}^m$ be a linear mapping and $T\in \mathbb{F}^{m\times n}$ be the equivalent matrix where $n=m$. The following conditions are equivalent.

1. $T()$ is bijective.
1. Columns of $T$ form a basis of $\mathbb{F}^m$.
1. $T$ is invertible.
1. For every $b\in \mathbb{F}^m$, $Tx=b$ has exactly one solution $x$.
</div>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Proof</summary>

The proof for each step is simply the combination of the proof for the corresponding step in injectivity and surjectivity.
</details>
